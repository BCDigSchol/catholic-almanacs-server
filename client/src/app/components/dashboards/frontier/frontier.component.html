Post offices as settlement. A post office opened where there were people to write letters, and closed when they left, so the post offices open in a year are the closest thing to a map of where Americans had settled by then. They come from Cameron Blevins and Richard W. Helbock's US Post Offices (Harvard Dataverse, 2021, CC0): 166,140 offices from 1639 to 2000 with the year each opened and closed. Two in three have a location (matched to the national place names); the rest, mostly short-lived country offices, are not on the map and not in the distances, so the settled country shown is a little thinner than it was. An office counts as open from 1 January of the year after it opened until the end of the year it closed.

The measures. For every institution the almanac placed, the tile build takes the straight-line distance to the nearest post office open on 1 January of that year, and whether the institution's first almanac listing comes before the year that office opened: "the church first". The almanacs begin in 1833 and skip 1839–1863, so a church founded earlier than its first listing can count as "after" when it was not, and the 1864 almanac lists many institutions for the first time that were founded in the gap; read the first-or-after shares as a floor. "Settled places with no church" are the open post offices with no almanac institution of that year within 25 miles, a sample of 500 of them on the map.

What it cannot show. Nearness is not cause: a mission far from any post office may have served a people the post never reached, and a post office with no church within 25 miles may have had a station the almanac did not print. The 1830s almanacs list far fewer places than those of the 1860s, so the early shares describe the almanac's reach as much as the Church's.
