Three readings of the center. The see of its diocese measures each institution from the cathedral of the diocese it was listed under (where no cathedral is placed, the middle of the diocese's institutions): the Church's own map of center and edge. The nearest metro area measures from the nearest of the 22 cities of 50,000 or more in 1870 (a boundary drawn for this site, not the period's; see any metro page). The circuit ties use no distance at all: a hub is a church whose clergy are recorded serving other places that year, a station a place served from elsewhere.

The rings are straight-line miles: within 5 (the city and its edge), 5–25 (a day's ride), 25–100 (the hinterland), and beyond. Institutions with no location are counted as remote. Each institution counts once a year.

Which way the clergy moved uses the resident posts: a priest who left one post for another is counted in the year he arrived, by whether the new post's ring is nearer the center than the old one's. Moves across the 1838–1864 gap in the almanacs are included and counted in 1864.

Primacy is the share of a center's institutions within 5 miles of it: high where a diocese was a city and little else, low where the see sat in a wide settled country. The 1830s almanacs record far fewer country places than those of the 1860s, so the early rings describe the almanac's reach as much as the Church's.
